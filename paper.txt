 Abstract—Multi-institutional collaboration in medical
 imaging faces major challenges: preserving patient privacy
 under HIPAA/GDPR regulations while enabling effective
 diagnostic model training across hospitals with diverse
 populations and imaging protocols. Existing federated
 learning approaches remain vulnerable to malicious par
ticipants and statistical heterogeneity, risking diagnostic
 accuracy in life-critical tasks such as Alzheimer’s disease
 detection. We introduce OptiGradTrust, a secure federated
 medical imaging framework that evaluates institutional
 gradient updates through a six-dimensional fingerprint,
 including VAE reconstruction error, cosine similarity, L2
 norm, sign-consistency, and Monte Carlo Shapley value.
 These indicators guide a hybrid reinforcement learning
attention module to adaptively assess trust. As part of
 this architecture, we integrate FedBN-Prox (FedBNP), which
 combines Federated Batch Normalization with proximal reg
ularization to improve convergence and diagnostic accuracy
 under heterogeneous clinical data. Extensive evaluations
 on Alzheimer’s MRI classification and standard benchmarks
 show that OptiGradTrust consistently outperforms existing
 defenses, achieving 97.24% diagnostic accuracy, while
 ensuring HIPAA-compliant privacy and robust resilience
 against diverse attack scenarios from compromised institu
tions.

 I. INTRODUCTION
 Alzheimer’s disease affects over 55 million people world
wide, and MRI-based early diagnosis is critical for timely
 intervention [1], [2]. Accurate deep learning models require
 large and diverse datasets that no single institution has, but
 privacy regulations (HIPAA, GDPR) prohibit the direct sharing
 of medical data [3], [4]. Federated Learning (FL) addresses this
 gap by allowing collaborative training in distributed hospitals
 while preserving patient privacy [5]–[7]. Recent advances also
 employ secure multi-party computation [8] and blockchain
 frameworks [9] for compliant medical data collaboration. As
 illustrated in Fig. 1, institutions exchange model updates rather

 Despite this promise, medical FL faces two key obstacles that
 threaten diagnostic accuracy and patient safety. First, Byzantine
 updates—malicious or low-quality gradient submissions—can
 severely degrade models and lead to misdiagnosis [10], [11].
 Such updates may arise from benign issues (e.g., scanner
 faults, poor calibration, limited labels, data preparation errors,
 insufficient local training) or from deliberate adversarial
 behavior at compromised institutions, introducing systematic
 bias into clinical decisions. Second, clinical data heterogene
ity—differences in scanners (1.5T vs. 3T), imaging protocols,
 patient demographics, and disease stages—creates severe non
IID distributions that undermine collaborative learning [1], [6],
 [12], [13].
 To address these limitations, we present OptiGradTrust, a
 unified trust framework that assigns each client update a com
prehensive six-dimensional fingerprint: (1) VAE reconstruction
 error (distributional anomalies), (2) cosine similarity to a trusted
 server reference (directional consistency), (3) average similarity
 to peer updates (consensus alignment), (4) L2 norm (magni
tude), (5) sign-consistency ratio (pattern agreement), and (6) a
 Monte Carlo Shapley value estimating marginal contribution
 to global validation performance. These metrics feed a hybrid
 RL–attention module that dynamically computes trust scores
 and adaptively reweights updates during aggregation, creating
 positive incentives for high-quality contributors. The complete
 pipeline is shown in Fig. 2.
 As part of the OptiGradTrust architecture, we integrate
 FedBN-Prox (FedBNP), which combines Federated Batch
 Normalization with proximal regularization to improve con
vergence under statistical heterogeneity and deliver superior
 accuracy–convergence trade-offs on Alzheimer’s MRI. We
 evaluate across MNIST, CIFAR-10, and Alzheimer’s MRI
 under five Byzantine attack types, achieving a +1.6 percentage
point improvement over FLGuard on Alzheimer’s MRI in
 Non-IID settings, while retaining > 97% accuracy on MNIST
 against FLTrust [14], FLAME [15], and FLGuard [16]. Beyond
 technical advances, OptiGradTrust promotes healthcare equity
 by enabling sophisticated diagnostics at sites with limited
 local data, benefiting underserved populations and rare-disease
 research where centralized approaches fail due to small cohorts.
 The framework follows regulatory best practices and supports
 near-term clinical deployment while laying foundations for
 next-generation medical AI infrastructure.

 II. RELATED WORK
 Medical federated learning enables hospitals to collab
oratively train diagnostic models while preserving patient
 privacy [1], [3]. However, real clinical deployment requires
 more than privacy: systems must maintain accuracy in the pres
ence of malicious clients, remain resilient to cross-institution
 heterogeneity, and adapt to evolving attack strategies [2],
 [4]. Early Byzantine-robust aggregators highlighted a core
 trade-off—greater robustness often came at the expense of
 accuracy. For example, Krum selects client updates based on
 geometric proximity [10] but has quadratic complexity and
 can exclude benign updates. Coordinate-wise robust statistics
 improve efficiency [17], yet remain vulnerable to sophisticated
 attacks. The geometric median (RobustFedAvg) [18] enhances
 robustness but still suffers accuracy drops under high attack
 ratios. Composite defenses such as Bulyan [19], tRFA [11],
 and FLAME [15] inherit the limitations of their components.
 Adaptive variants like RL-GM [20] dynamically adjust weights,
 but they primarily focus on aggregation rather than broader op
timization challenges. These approaches become even harder to
 tune under non-IID medical data, where the accuracy–security
 trade-off becomes sharper.
 In parallel, heterogeneity-focused optimizers aim to stabilize
 learning under non-IID distributions, though they typically
 assume honest participants. FedAvg [5] serves as the baseline
 but struggles under distribution shifts. FedProx constrains
 client drift with a proximal term [21], while SCAFFOLD
 reduces variance via control variates [22]. FedADMM leverages
 consensus optimization [23], and FedNova normalizes updates
 to address local objective imbalance [24]. FedDWA dynamically
 adjusts aggregation weights [25], while adaptive methods such
 as FedAdam [26] accelerate convergence. For medical imaging,
 FedBN keeps BatchNorm layers local [27], yielding notable
 gains (e.g., > 6% on Alzheimer’s MRI), though it offers no
 protection against adversarial clients. These optimizers improve
 convergence and stability but remain vulnerable when attackers
 are present.
 Beyond single defenses, multi-signal systems integrate mul
tiple indicators to improve robustness. FLTrust [14] compares
 client updates to a trusted reference gradient but requires
 clean server data rarely available in healthcare. FedSV uses
 Shapley values for data valuation [28], [29] but incurs high
 computational cost. Gradient-pattern detectors such as VAE
 reconstructions [30], SignGuard [31], and other representation
based methods offer additional protection, yet often rely on
 single indicators. Differential privacy mechanisms [4] and
 adaptive IoT-based monitoring [13] mitigate specific risks but
 do not provide comprehensive resilience. Fusion defenses like
 FeRA [32], FLGuard [16], SAFEFL [33], and BatFL [34]
 combine multiple signals, but static fusion rules remain vul
nerable to adaptive adversaries who gradually learn thresholds.
 Time-staged attacks can remain undetected, underscoring the
 need for dynamic and adaptive trust strategies.
 Medical applications amplify these challenges: patient out
comes depend on diagnostic accuracy, data distributions vary
 across hospitals, and even a single compromised site can
 severely impact results. Studies show that standard federated
 learning can lose up to nine percentage points under demo
graphic and protocol shifts [6], while targeted backdoors can
 drive misdiagnosis rates above 98% [8], [9]. Despite these
 risks, clinical collaborations remain promising: federated tumor
 segmentation achieves 86% Dice across five hospitals [35],
 and federated Alzheimer’s diagnosis reaches 85% accuracy
 across institutions [36]. The ecosystem extends to personalized
 HAR[12], EHR analysis [7], DP-protected imaging [4], and IoT
 health monitoring [13]. In practice, variations in demographics,

 A. OptiGradTrust: Bridging Critical Gaps
 OptiGradTrust addresses these challenges by jointly tackling
 robustness, heterogeneity, and adaptivity through four inte
grated innovations. First, FedBNP combines FedBN’s localized
 BatchNorm with FedProx’s proximal regularization, ensuring
 stable performance under both heterogeneity and Byzantine
 attacks. Unlike FedBN and FedProx which exist independently,
 FedBNP is proposed in this work as a novel hybrid optimizer
 within the OptiGradTrust framework. Second, a six-dimensional
 gradient fingerprint integrates complementary trust indicators:
 VAE reconstruction error, cosine similarity (to both trusted
 reference and peer consensus), L2 norm, sign-consistency ratio,
 and Monte Carlo Shapley values [37]—making evasion far
 more difficult. Third, dual attention dynamically weights these
 indicators by context, replacing static fusion [24]. Finally, a
 reinforcement learning policy continuously adapts trust strate
gies to evolving adversaries [20]. Together, these components
 unify optimizer stability, anomaly detection, adaptive weighting,
 and policy learning—providing robust defense for life-critical
 medical imaging.
 Table I compares representative methods, highlighting how
 OptiGradTrust closes long-standing gaps. Complementary
 research explores improved convergence (e.g., matched averag
ing [24]) and backdoor vulnerabilities in federated GAN-based
 medical synthesis [38], reinforcing the need for comprehensive
 and adaptive defenses in healthcare federated learning.

 V. DISCUSSION
 Our evaluation reveals significant advantages formulti
institutionalmedical collaboration,demonstratinghowOpti
GradTrustaddressescriticalchallenges infederatedmedical
 imagingwhilemaintaining patient privacy and diagnostic
 accuracy.
 A.ClinicalPerformanceandMedicalDeployment
 OptiGradTrust delivers clinicallymeaningful diagnostic
 performance.OnAlzheimer’sMRIitachieves97.24%accuracy
 whilemaintainingstrongresultsonstandardbenchmarks(e.g.,
 MNIST99.41%), which is criticalwheremisclassification
 candirectlyaffect treatment decisions.Duringdeployment,
 progressivelearningshiftssecurityfromstaticrulestoadaptive
 behavior: thesystemcontinuallyrefines itsability todetect
 compromisedinstitutionsorequipmentmalfunctions,andtrust
weightedaggregationreplacesbinaryexclusionbyproportion
allydown-weightingsuspiciousupdates,preservingdiversity
 fromunderrepresentedpopulationswhileminimizingpotential
 harm; inourevaluations, thisraisesdetectionprecisionfrom
 27.59%(baseline) to as high as 100%. Clinical overheads
 remainmanageable: roughly 40%computational and 11%
 communicationoverheadduringtrainingrepresentareasonable
 trade-off insafety-critical settings, and the systemsustains
 diagnosticstabilityunder severeclinical heterogeneitywith
 onlya3.98%accuracydegradationinthemostextremenon-IID
 scenarioduetoFedBNP’sabilitytoaccommodateinstitution
specificstatisticswhilepreservingsecurity.
 B.ClinicalLimitations,RegulatoryPathways,andFuture
 Directions
 Whilepromising, several considerations remainforwide
scaleclinicaladoption.First, scalability:O(N2)components
 canchallengeverylargeconsortiawithhundredsofinstitutions.
 Second,hyperparametersensitivity:domain-specifictuningis
 neededacross imagingmodalitiesandpopulations,motivating
 automatedtuning.Third,novelattacks:althoughprogressive
 learningadapts toknown threats, zero-day, system-targeted
 attacks require further adversarial testing. Fourth, resource
 demands:computeandcommunicationoverheadmayburden
 resource-constrainedsites, suggestinglightweightdeployment
 paths.
 Froma regulatoryandvalidationperspective, privacy-by
design(norawdatasharing,optionaldifferentialprivacy)aligns
 withGDPRArticle25,whileinterpretabletrustscoresandaudit
 trailssupportemergingFDAAI/MLandEUMDRexpectations
 for transparency.Clinical validationcan followprospective
 studiesdemonstratingnon-inferioritytocentralizedtraining,
 especiallyfor rarediseaseswherefederatedapproachesbring
 uniquebenefits.
 Futuredirections includemulti-agent reinforcement learning
 for coordinated threat detection across hospital networks;
 continual adaptation to evolving cyber-threats in clinical
 environments;hierarchicalarchitectures tosupport thousands
 of institutions forglobal initiatives;3DvolumetricCT/MRI
 modeling for conditions requiring spatial context;medical
specificgradientcompressionforbandwidth-limitednetworks;
 andstandards-basedintegrationwithclinicaldecisionsupport
 systems toensureinteroperability.
